Acquisitions

The first 90 days after a concept joins the group

Big Beautiful Brain · October 2026 · 7 minute read

When a concept joins a new group, the regulars do not read the announcement. They notice whether the bread is the same, whether their server is still there and whether the kitchen still gets their order right. The first ninety days decide whether they keep coming back, and those are the days when the group knows least about what made the place work.

No margin for a slow start

Hospitality groups transact like investors and operate on margins most investors would not accept. The National Restaurant Association puts the median pre-tax margin for full-service restaurants at 2.8% of sales in 2024, down from 4% in 2019, and 4% for limited-service, down from 6%. Total costs for an average restaurant rose 36% between 2019 and 2026, and 42% of operators said their restaurant was not profitable in 2025.

At those margins, a transition that lets food cost drift by a point or two, loses a sous chef or leaves event inquiries unanswered for a few weeks can take the venue from profit to loss before the new partners have finished learning where the walk-in is.

Why growth by acquisition goes wrong

The industry has recent, public examples of what happens when acquisition outruns integration. In its February 2026 investor presentation filed with the SEC, Jack in the Box wrote that it "was more focused on scaling via M&A causing underlying operations to suffer". It divested Del Taco in October 2025 for $119 million, less than four years after acquiring it for $575 million, and is closing restaurants that average $1.2 million in sales and lose $70,000 a year at the four-wall level.

Integration is not the paperwork after the deal. In restaurants it is where the deal is won or lost, and even the best acquirers budget heavily for it. When Darden agreed to acquire Chuy's in 2024 for about $605 million, it expected roughly $15 million a year in synergies and $50 million to $55 million in acquisition and integration costs. Most groups adding their fourth or fourteenth venue do not have Darden's integration team, and they face the same problem at smaller scale.

What lives in people, not in the books

A seller's P&L, POS exports and leases describe what a restaurant earned. They say little about how it earned it. That knowledge sits with a handful of people, and in this industry people move quickly. Black Box Intelligence measured management turnover in the third quarter of 2024 at 38% a year in full-service restaurants and 55% in limited-service, and hourly turnover at 96% and 135%. It put the hard cost of replacing a general manager at $16,770. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a quits rate in accommodation and food services of 4.2% a month in 2025, twice the all-industry rate.

When the chef or the GM leaves in the first months after a sale, these leave with them:

  • Recipes, specs, prep lists and par levels, and the small changes that made a dish work.
  • Supplier relationships, the terms nobody wrote down and who to call when a delivery is short.
  • How the POS is really configured, and the workarounds the team uses every shift.
  • Who the regulars are, what they order and which ones bring their companies for events.
  • Which staff hold the place together, and which ones are already looking.
  • Lease side letters, landlord history and the repairs that keep being put off.

Days 1 to 30: capture before anything changes

Spend the first month learning, not fixing. Interview the chef, the GM, the bar lead and the people on the line, ideally recorded with consent, and walk lunch, dinner and the close. Pull the POS, inventory, invoice, schedule, payroll and review history into one place, and reconcile it, so the numbers agree before anyone acts on them. Capture recipes and routines while the people who own them are still there. Every fact should carry its source, so that later decisions can be traced back to the interview or the export they rest on.

Days 31 to 60: decide with evidence

With a cited picture of the venue, the hard decisions get easier to make and to explain. Compare its prime cost, labor and menu mix with the group's other venues on the same terms. Decide who to keep, promote and replace on the evidence of who actually holds the operation together. Map suppliers and contracts onto the group's own and decide what to consolidate. Rank the quick wins by value, confidence and effort, and agree owners and dates for each.

Days 61 to 90: put it to work

By the third month the venue should be running inside the group, not beside it. Prime cost, labor and guest feedback should be reported the same way as every other venue. Event and private dining inquiries should be answered in minutes and followed through, so the revenue the founders built is kept rather than lost in the handover. A weekly readout should show the partners what has changed and what has not.

The knowledge should outlast the transition

The groups that absorb acquisitions well treat what they learn in the first ninety days as an asset to keep, not a project to finish. When it lives in a brain that agents keep current, it survives the next departure, it makes the next acquisition faster to absorb, and it is there, organized and cited, when a buyer asks how the group really works.
