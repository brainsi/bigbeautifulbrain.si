Persistent agents

Agents that stay

Big Beautiful Brain · October 2026 · 8 minute read

Most AI deployed at work is built for a moment: a question, a draft, a summary. The value in a business is created over journeys that last months: a founder courted before there is a sale process, an add-on absorbed into a platform, a corporate client who books with you every December, a wedding planned a year ahead. Those journeys are where agents can do the most good, and where most AI tools fall short.

Where value is won and lost

High-value journeys share a shape. They run for a long time, involve many people on both sides, and are decided by follow-through. Bain says the best private equity firms track the companies they want to own "over years, not months". Corporate buyers take about ten months to make a purchase, according to 6sense's 2025 survey of 3,744 buyers. The average US wedding in 2025 cost $34,000 and involved thirteen vendors, according to The Knot.

Along the way, small lapses decide the outcome. A study of more than 15,000 leads led by Professor Oldroyd at MIT found that the odds of qualifying a lead fell 21-fold when the first response slipped from five minutes to thirty. Cvent's 2026 planner survey found that 39% of planners of small corporate events write off a venue that has not replied within two days. Across more than six million events booked through its platform in 2025 and 2026, Tripleseat reports that about 82 of every 100 inquiries did not convert.

Keeping a relationship is usually worth more than winning a new one. Harvard Business Review, citing Fred Reichheld of Bain, reports that a 5% increase in retention lifts profit by 25% to 95%, and that acquiring a customer costs five to 25 times as much as keeping one.

Why most AI does not help here

The gap is not intelligence. It is memory. MIT NANDA's 2025 study of enterprise AI found that for multi-week work such as managing a client, 90% of people still prefer a human, because most AI tools "do not retain feedback, adapt to context, or improve over time". Its authors put it plainly: "The dividing line isn't intelligence, it's memory, adaptability, and learning capability."

The results show it. BCG found in 2025 that 60% of companies report hardly any material value from AI. Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027, over rising costs, unclear business value and weak risk controls. Deloitte's 2026 survey of 3,235 leaders found that only one company in five has a mature model for governing autonomous agents.

What changed in 2025 and 2026

The technology for agents that stay with a journey now exists. METR measures the length of task an AI agent can complete on its own and reports that it has doubled about every 4.3 months since 2023. The major cloud platforms shipped long-term agent memory between July 2025 and April 2026. AWS now keeps a single agent session alive for up to fourteen days, and durable execution platforms such as Temporal report customers running agents "for days, weeks, or months" that pause for a person's approval and pick up where they left off. The Model Context Protocol, now governed by the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation, gives agents a standard way to reach the systems a business runs on.

What remains hard is everything around the model: knowing the business well enough to act in it, remembering a journey accurately for months, knowing when to stop and ask, and leaving a record an owner can trust.

What a persistent agent needs

  • A cited picture of the business. An agent that answers an event inquiry needs the real rooms, menus, minimums and dates. One that tracks an add-on needs the platform's customers, systems and contracts. Without that context it produces answers that sound right and are not.
  • Memory of the journey. Every message, promise, preference and decision since the first contact, kept with its source, so nothing depends on one person remembering.
  • Events to wake on. An email, a booking, a signed document, a date in the calendar or a change in a connected system, so the agent acts when something happens rather than when someone remembers to ask.
  • Rules for when to ask a person. Which offers it may make, which steps need approval, and when a relationship needs a human voice. People should be told when they are dealing with an assistant; in the European Union that has been a legal requirement since August 2026.
  • A record of every step. What it did, what it relied on and who approved it, so the owner, a lender or a buyer can check the work.

Where to start

Pick one journey where the value is clear and follow-through is measurable. In a restaurant group, that is often event and private dining inquiries, or the corporate accounts that order every month. In private equity, it may be the add-on being absorbed this quarter, the key accounts at one portfolio company, or the readiness work ahead of a sale. Measure what matters before and after: response time, conversion, rebooking, retention, the time it takes to answer a buyer's question. Then add the next journey.

MIT found that AI projects built with an outside partner reached deployment about twice as often as internal builds. That is the case for an AI-native services firm: a partner who builds the brain, runs the agents, keeps a person in the loop and is measured on what the journey delivers. That is the work we do, for private equity firms and for restaurant and hospitality groups.
