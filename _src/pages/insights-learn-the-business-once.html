Operating knowledge

Stop paying to learn the same business twice

Big Beautiful Brain · October 2026 · 5 minute read

Over the life of a deal, a company is studied again and again. Diligence advisers map it before signing, consultants map it again in the first hundred days, interim leaders learn it a third time, and a buyer's advisers study it once more at exit. Each group builds an understanding of the business, and most of that understanding leaves with them.

Paying for the same understanding more than once

The deliverable from most of this work is a document: a report, a deck or a model. Documents are useful on the day they are delivered, but they do not update themselves, they rarely connect to each other, and they sit in folders the next team may never open. A firm can pay $500,000 to $1 million for a top-tier adviser to interview a target's customers and write a long report (TechCrunch, March 2026), and within a year the company is paying again to learn what it already knew.

Understanding as something you maintain

Our approach changes what an owner gets for the money, from a deliverable to something the company keeps. The knowledge of the business is captured once in the first two weeks, in the same structure for every company, cited to its sources and checked by the people who run the business. After that it is maintained like any important asset: every meeting, document and decision adds to it, nothing is overwritten without a record of who changed it and why, and every quarter we measure whether it is making the business better.

What makes operating knowledge worth keeping

  • Provenance. Every fact points to where it came from, so it can be trusted and checked.
  • Review. People approve what enters the record, so it reflects how the business really works.
  • Structure. A consistent shape lets a sponsor read every portfolio company the same way.
  • Access. Sensitive knowledge is protected fact by fact, so it can be shared widely without exposing what should stay private.
  • Portability. It belongs to the company, exports at any time and transfers with it at exit.

What it means at exit

A business that has kept its knowledge current through the hold arrives at a sale with its history already organized: what changed, why, what it delivered and the evidence behind each number. Vendor diligence draws on facts that already exist, buyer questions are answered with sources, and the new owner starts from a far better place than the one the seller had, which is worth something in the price.
