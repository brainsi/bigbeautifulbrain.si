Why AI has not paid off in the portfolio yet

Big Beautiful Brain · October 2026 · 5 minute read

Portfolio companies are being encouraged to adopt AI everywhere: assistants for every employee, agents for finance and customer service, copilots inside every system. The results so far are thin. In S&P Global Market Intelligence's 2026 private equity survey, 75 percent of respondents rated AI ineffective for portfolio monitoring, even as 72 percent named operational improvement their most important lever for creating value.

Capability without context

An agent asked to chase late invoices needs to know which customers have negotiated terms, which ones are disputing a shipment and who owns each relationship. An assistant asked about a process needs to know how the process really runs, not how a three-year-old procedure document says it runs. Without that context, even a strong model produces answers that sound right and are not, and teams learn to stop trusting it.

Context first

The fix is to give every AI tool the same reliable picture of the company before asking it to do anything important. Once the business has been captured, with every fact cited and reviewed, an assistant or agent can query that picture directly through MCP, its answers can be grounded in the evidence behind them, and the same picture can feed the dashboards where people already work. Access levels apply to every path, so an agent sees only what the person it serves may see.

Agents that make the picture better

When an agent learns something new, from a meeting, a document or a change in a system, it proposes the update with its source, and a person decides whether it counts. Over time the agents improve the record and the record improves the agents, while the people who run the business stay in control of what it says.

A practical order of work

  • Capture the business first, so there is context worth connecting to.
  • Start with the questions leadership asks most often, and measure how well they are answered.
  • Connect the AI tools the company already uses before buying new ones.
  • Let agents propose changes through review, and widen what they do as trust builds.
  • Measure effectiveness every quarter, and keep what works.
